EPA 608 Technician Certification: Requirements, Exam & Step-by-Step Path

Under Section 608 of the Clean Air Act, technicians who maintain, service, repair, or dispose of equipment that could release refrigerants into the atmosphere must be certified.

Quick Answer

The bottom line

EPA Section 608 certification is the federal credential required before any technician opens a refrigerant circuit on stationary air-conditioning, heat-pump, or refrigeration equipment. You choose a type — Core plus Type I (small appliances), Type II (high-pressure), Type III (low-pressure), or Universal (all three) — pass a 25-question multiple-choice section per type at 70%, and you are certified for life. No degree and no classes are required; many technicians self-study and test for $20–$90.

Legally required to handle refrigerants — penalties exceed $44,000/day without it
No education prerequisite; self-study is enough
Certification never expires and needs no renewal or CE
Universal card lets you work on any stationary equipment
Low one-time cost ($20–$90)

The Exam in Detail

EPA 608 is organized into a mandatory Core section plus up to three Type sections. Universal certification means Core + all three Types. Each section is 25 multiple-choice questions; you need 70% (18/25) on each, scored independently:

What’s on the EPA 608 exam

Core (mandatory for all)

  • Ozone depletion & the Clean Air Act
  • Venting prohibition & recovery/recycle/reclaim
  • Refrigerants, oils & leak-rate thresholds
  • Safe handling & person certification
  • A2L low-GWP refrigerants (R-454B, R-32)

Type I — Small appliances

  • Equipment with ≤5 lb original charge
  • Leak repair & verification
  • Recovery & evacuation procedures
  • Type I appliance rules

Type II — High-pressure

  • Splits, rooftop units (R-410A, R-22)
  • High-pressure leak-rate standards
  • Recovery & evacuation requirements
  • MVAC-like equipment

Type III — Low-pressure

  • Centrifugal & low-pressure chillers
  • Recovery & purge procedures
  • Evacuation & leak repair
  • Low-pressure specific rules

Universal = Core + I + II + III

  • 100 questions total (four 25-Q sections)
  • Most employers prefer Universal
  • Take sections together or separately

Frequently Asked Questions

What is EPA 608 certification?
It is the federal credential required under Section 608 of the Clean Air Act before a technician may open a refrigerant circuit on stationary AC, heat-pump, or refrigeration equipment.
What is the difference between Type I, II, III, and Universal?
Type I covers small appliances (≤5 lb), Type II high-pressure systems (splits, rooftop units), Type III low-pressure chillers. Universal is Core plus all three and is what most employers want.
How many questions are on the exam and what is the passing score?
Each section is 25 multiple-choice questions; you need 70% (18 correct) per section. Universal is 100 questions total across the four sections.
Does EPA 608 certification expire?
No. Under federal law it is permanent — there is no expiration, renewal, or continuing education.
Do I need experience or classes to get EPA 608?
No. There is no education or experience prerequisite; many technicians self-study and test for $20–$90.
How much does it cost?
About $20–$35 per section through an EPA-approved organization; free testing is available from some providers (e.g., SkillCat). Universal typically runs $25–$90.
What is the difference between EPA 608 and 609?
Section 608 covers stationary refrigeration and AC; Section 609 is a separate credential for motor-vehicle air conditioning. They do not substitute for each other.
Can I take EPA 608 online or for free?
Yes. Many approved organizations offer online proctored testing, and some (like SkillCat) offer the training and exam free via a mobile app.
What are A2L refrigerants and are they on the exam?
A2L refrigerants (R-454B, R-32) are the new low-GWP replacements being phased in as R-410A is retired. EPA 608 now includes A2L handling and safety, so they appear on current exams.
Is EPA 608 required by law?
Yes for the work it covers — technicians who handle refrigerants without it face penalties exceeding $44,000 per day under the Clean Air Act.
